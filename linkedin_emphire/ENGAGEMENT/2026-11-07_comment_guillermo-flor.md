# Comment on Guillermo Flor's post on the thirty-day silicon loop, 7 November 2026

## What the record says

The post reports a research fab at Giga Texas and a production Terafab in Grimes County, covering logic,
memory, lithography, packaging and test so a full design-make-test loop can run on roughly a thirty-day
clock, and draws the twelve-swings-a-year figure from that. The claim is his, sourced to the reporting,
and nothing in the comment disputes it.

Two comments were already on the post when this was written. Shawn Bullock took the evidence angle at
length, down to failed chips preserving defects and thermal behaviour into the next cycle. Claire Zhu took
the plain speed angle. Both are fine and both are taken, which is why this one answers the question the
post actually ends on instead of adding a third version of the same point.

## Draft, paste as a comment

Answering the question first: none of the three, most weeks. The fab queue sets the ceiling, and the
review loop sets the pace.

Twelve swings a year is a research programme only if each swing changes one variable and the outcome can
be attributed. Let everything move at once and you get twelve correlated samples rather than twelve
lessons. Moore's law was a schedule; the compounding came from the discipline around each step, not from
the clock.

Fabs already keep lot genealogy for precisely this reason. The thin part is the review, where a lot that
failed qualification and a lot that was deprioritised can leave the same trace, so the next cycle
inherits the ambiguity.
