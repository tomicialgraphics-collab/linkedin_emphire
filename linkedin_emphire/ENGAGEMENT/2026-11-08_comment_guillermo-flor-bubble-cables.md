# Comment on Guillermo Flor's AI bubble post, 8 November 2026

## Why a second comment, and why this angle

His last post was about a thirty-day silicon loop and the comment went at iteration speed against learning rate. This one
is about what survives a bubble, so nothing is being repeated.

The argument he makes is correct and slightly too clean, and the honest addition is the part of the dot-com mechanism that
does not transfer. Fibre survived because it sat in the ground at zero marginal cost and anyone could buy it cheap. AI
capacity does not sit still: it depreciates, it needs power, and it needs people to keep running it. So the inheritance is
real but it is made of different material than the analogy suggests.

## Comment

The cables survived because nobody had to keep paying for them. That is the part of the analogy that does not transfer.

Fibre sat in the ground at almost no marginal cost for years. Compute depreciates on a much shorter clock than glass, and a
data centre that survives a bankruptcy still has a power bill. So what is actually inheritable here is not the hardware. It
is the people who learned to run it, and the evaluation practice they built while doing it, and neither of those appears
anywhere in the write-down.

Which is the better news for builders, and the reason the next wave tends to be built by people who were in the room when
the last one broke.

## What not to say

No disagreement with his conclusion, because he is right that the inventions do not disappear. No specific depreciation
figures or overbuild numbers, which would be the easiest way to be confidently wrong under an investor's post. No mention
of my own work.

## Held for a later turn

If he replies, the question worth asking is what an investor should underwrite instead of infrastructure when the durable
asset is people and practice, since that does not go on sale in a bankruptcy either.
