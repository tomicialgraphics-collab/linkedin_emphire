A SOC 2 says nothing about what the model will say.

The AI vendor you approved last year was approved on a form that never asked it a model question.

A security questionnaire is a good instrument pointed at the wrong target. You can score a vendor highly and still have no idea what their model does when a user asks it to forget its instructions.

Here's what most teams get wrong about this:

They treat AI vendors as a procurement problem, so the review inherits the shape of procurement. Certificates, subprocessors, data residency. All necessary, and none of it a control on behaviour.

The reality is different.

A SOC 2 attests that controls exist and were audited. It says nothing about whether the model refuses what it should refuse, or whether the guardrail holds in a second language. Behaviour is a property of the system, and it changes when the model updates, usually without anything telling you.

Here's what actually works:

→ Ask for the eval report, not the certificate. Ten prompts with their expected refusals tells you more than a forty-page policy.

→ Run three of those prompts yourself before renewal. Vendor evals are self-graded, and self-graded tests pass.

→ Get a model-change clause into the contract. If behaviour can shift silently, the last review is the only thing you control.

The part nobody tells you:

Vendors who answer this well tend to be small, and they answer fast because the tests already exist. Vendors who send a deck instead are usually wrapping someone else's model, and nobody has written the tests.

Your register is only as current as the last time you asked.
