## Comment, paste as is

The number I'd watch isn't the subscription, it's the review. Karen drafts, Stewie checks, you read every email before it goes out, which is the right chain while the volume is small. The question is what happens at forty, because the agents' throughput sets your reading load and your reading doesn't scale with it.

The tell is a reject rate that sits at zero for a month. At that point the approval is a keystroke, the control has quietly become a formality, and it's harder to spot than an unread queue because somebody did look.

What I'd do on that budget is classify by blast radius rather than reviewing everything. Output that leaves the building gets read. Output that stays inside gets sampled, and the sample gets a date on it. You keep the same $200 team without paying for it in evening hours nobody budgeted.

The security side is harder, because a briefing nobody read looks exactly like a briefing that was right.

## Why the review and not the cost

The episode's headline is the price, and the price is genuinely surprising, so every comment will be about the price. The thing that decides whether the team is safe is not what it costs but who reads what it produces, and that is the part the recording leaves as an assumption.

Mike is honest that the design depends on him reading every outbound email. That is a real control and it is also a control with a throughput limit that the agents do not share. A team is only as supervised as its least-read queue, and the queue that matters is the one that grew after the design was set.

## Why a zero reject rate is the signal

A review that never rejects anything is either reviewing work that never needs it or not reviewing. Both look identical from outside, and the second looks like success from inside, which is why the metric has to be on the reviewer rather than on the output.

It also gives an owner something to check this week without buying anything: how many of the last twenty approvals were sent back, and how long each one took. Two numbers, no tooling.

## Why classify by blast radius

Per-item review of everything is the instinct and it is the thing that fails first, because it competes with the actual job. Splitting output into what leaves the building and what does not keeps the strict check where the harm is and turns the rest into a sampled audit with a date, which is weaker and honest rather than strong and fictional.

## What this is not

No link, no offer, no mention of engagements. The post is a show promotion with a giveaway template, and the audience is owners who have no budget, so a comment that sells would be read as the reason the free thing is free.

Not a criticism of Mike's setup. He says on the recording that this never replaces a real team, and the comment takes him at his word rather than arguing with it.

## If either of them replies

The question worth having is what the sample size should be for the internal output, because a monthly sample of five tells you almost nothing and a monthly sample of fifty is the same work as reading everything. The honest answer is probably that sampling suits the briefing and nothing else, and the rest needs a rule rather than a reviewer.
