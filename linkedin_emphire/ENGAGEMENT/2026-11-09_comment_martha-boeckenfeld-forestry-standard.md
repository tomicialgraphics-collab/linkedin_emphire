## Comment, paste as is

Sweden passed the obligation in 1903 and the measurement in 1923. The rule is what people remember, but the figures in this post only exist because somebody started walking plots twenty years later.

That ordering is the transferable part, and it is the reverse of how AI governance usually works. Principles get written first, then a metric is found that flatters them, and the metric arrives late or never. Here the obligation was specific enough to measure from the start: a stand, a fell, three years.

Most AI governance cannot be checked that way yet. It names an owner and a cadence rather than a system, an act and a date. The question worth putting to a board is not whether it has a policy. It is whether a stranger could verify one clause of it without asking anyone in the building.

## What not to say

Do not argue that the analogy fails. It does not fail, it gets sharper, and the correction is in her own sources rather than against them. No mention of the playbook, no percentage of AI policies that cannot be checked, and no claim about Swedish law beyond what the post itself cites.

## If she replies

The follow-on is what a checkable clause looks like in practice: one named system, one prohibited action, one date by which the evidence has to exist, and one named holder of the evidence. That is a paragraph, not a thread, and it is the shape of the audit offering.
