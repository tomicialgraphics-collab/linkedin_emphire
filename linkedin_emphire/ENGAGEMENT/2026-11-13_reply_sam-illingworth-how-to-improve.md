## The angle

He asked a real question, so the reply gives him something he can put in front of a procurement committee rather than
more of the argument he already agreed with. Three moves, each of which needs no new model and no lab: make the
summary declare what it left out, mine the caseworker's edits as free error labels, and run the closed-referral test
on fifty cases instead of a hundred.

The last line is the only opinion in it, and it is the one that matters: the technical work is small, and what is
missing is a decision that omission counts as a number worth holding.

## Reply, paste as is

Start with what a summary cannot say about itself. Require it to carry the evidence it did not use, the documents, sections and statements it set aside, so the reader sees the outline of the gap instead of assuming there is none. It costs a field, and it changes what the summary is claiming to be.

Their casework already holds the next signal. Every time a caseworker edits or rejects a summary, that edit is a free label for what the summary got wrong. Log them, group them by type, and an error profile appears without anyone commissioning an evaluation.

Then run the closed-referral test small rather than perfectly. Fifty cases, a second reader who sees the summary alone, one question about which fact decided the case.

What holds it together is a decision rather than a model: that omission is a number the record has to carry.

## What not to say

No thanks-for-asking, no restatement of the argument he just agreed with, and no variation on "happy to help" or an
offer of a call. No mention of the playbook, no link, no hashtags. Nothing that turns his question into a lead. The
three moves have to be specific enough that he could hand them to a committee, or the reply is just agreement with
better formatting.
