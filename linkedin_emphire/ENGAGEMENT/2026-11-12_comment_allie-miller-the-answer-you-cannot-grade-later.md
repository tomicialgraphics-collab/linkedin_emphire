## Comment, paste as is

An agent reads a vendor contract, decides the indemnity clause is boilerplate, and moves on. That call looks exactly like every good call it made that morning, and nobody finds out for a year, when the clause turns into the dispute.

Your examples all have something the interesting cases don't: you can grade them in a minute. Zillow is obviously the right place to look. A short deck is obviously right for a quick update to your boss. Being wrong costs you an afternoon at most, so the judgment looks reasonable from where you are sitting.

The agent work I get asked about doesn't work like that. Before its answer reaches a client, I want the reviewer's own answer in the ticket before they open the agent's. Then a disagreement is worth arguing about. A year of agreement means either the agent is doing the work or nobody is reviewing.

Without that, nobody can say afterwards whether the judgment was better than the person's, which is the question that turns up after something has gone wrong.

## Why this one

The post is an argument about a word, and the thread has split along the usual line: half of it agrees that AI shows judgment, the other half says judgment needs a stakeholder who can be blamed. Both are talking past the useful part.

Her examples share a property she does not name. Zillow against a junk site, a short deck for a quick update, an emoji count matched to the sender: each one is gradeable within a minute, by the person who asked, because the outcome is visible and cheap to reverse. That is why the argument is easy to win, and it is also why the examples do not settle anything about the judgments that get organisations into trouble.

The version I work on has a delayed outcome and no counterfactual. A clause dismissed as boilerplate is indistinguishable from a clause read correctly until the dispute, and by then the only artefact left is the output. Recording the reviewer's own answer before they see the agent's is unglamorous, takes one field in a ticket, and it is the only thing that turns "the AI showed good judgment" into something anyone can check afterwards.

- Azhar Tahir and Kason Kentner have the failure-mode answer: a confident wrong call looks like a good one, and an agent misreported its own state when an MCP had disconnected. Kason's approval inbox is the same instinct as the field this comment asks for, applied to permission rather than to grading.
- Oleg Peker has the negative-claim case, where "this does not exist" is the hardest thing to check. That is a sibling of this point and not the same one.
- Bill McCormick has the legal standard, and Paul Stevens has the exclusions nobody notices. Both are better stated there than I could state them, so the comment does not restate them.

Writing a second comment, or replying to any of the above, would be a fourth voice making the third point. One comment, one idea, and it stays in the lane the account can defend: what evidence survives the decision.
