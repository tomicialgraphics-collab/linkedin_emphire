## Comment, paste as is

The question that decides this isn't whether they're related. It's whether any of those "no evidence of an attack" lines came from a search that could have found one.

Chase's technical fault, the FAA's network equipment, Zelle's operational network: each is a conclusion drawn by the party whose service failed, published as a summary rather than the evidence behind it. A negative finding is only worth what the retained material was worth. If the logs that would have shown an intrusion had rolled off before anyone went looking, "no evidence" means there was nothing left to find.

So the useful question for each of the six is dull and specific. What was retained, for how long, and does anybody outside the operator hold a copy? Where the answer is thirty days of the operator's own logs and nothing independent, the attack hypothesis wasn't tested. It was declined.

Which is the third option, and it's worse than either of his. Not attacked, not decaying, but unable to tell afterwards.

## Why the negative claim and not the timeline

The timeline is the part everyone will argue about, and it is unfalsifiable from outside: six incidents, several attributed to technical causes by the organisations that would have to detect an intrusion, and no reader in a position to check any of them. A comment that ranks the plausibility of each would be invention dressed as analysis.

The one thing worth saying is about the sentence that carries the weight in every case, which is the negative. "No evidence of an attack" is read by most readers as "not an attack". It actually reports the state of somebody's search, and a search is only as good as what was still on disk when it started.

## Where this is answerable and where it is not

It is answerable for the operator: retention policy, log integrity, whether anything is shipped off the estate and where. Those are documented facts inside most regulated organisations and are the first thing an auditor asks for.

It is not answerable from outside, which is why his question about whether we would know has an uncomfortable answer. The public reporting can only tell you what an operator chose to say about its own search, and an operator that never looked can say the same sentence as one that looked and found nothing.

## What this is not

No link, no offer, no mention of engagements. The post is a question, not a claim, and the comment answers the question rather than joining a speculation.

Not a suggestion that these were attacks. It argues that the evidence published so far cannot rule one out, which is a weaker and more uncomfortable statement than either side of the argument will want.

## If he replies

The question worth having is whether the FAA's five hours produced anything retained and independently held, because that is the one incident with a regulator in the loop who publishes. Air traffic control is the rare case where the negative claim should be checkable rather than asserted.
