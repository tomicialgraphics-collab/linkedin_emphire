## Comment, paste as is

The one process that earned its keep immediately, for me: a single line per deliverable saying what would prove it's done. Not a doc, not a review, one sentence a stranger could check.

The ones I'd introduce late, or never: templates before a second person uses the template, and approvals before anyone has made the mistake the approval prevents. A review that exists before the burn teaches nobody anything, and it slows the person who already knows.

The test I use now, running this as a one-person practice rather than a company, is blunter: if I can't name the failure a process prevents and the last time it happened, I don't add it. Written rules are memory of a specific burn. No burn, nothing to remember.

Which is the uncomfortable half of your brain analogy. A brain that records every instruction it was ever given isn't smart, it's just full. The useful question isn't what did Google do, it's what did Google know that you now know too, and what did it do because a thousand people needed a shared answer to a question you don't have yet.

## Why this answers her question rather than her topic

She asks two specific things at the end: what helped, what came too early. A comment that discusses Google's engineering culture answers neither.

So the first line answers the first question with one process, and the second answers the second with two. The rest is the test behind both, which is the part she can use on the next decision rather than this one.

## The honesty constraint

The owner has not built an early-stage company. Claiming otherwise under a founder's post, in front of an audience that includes other founders, is the fastest way to be caught and the worst way to be caught.

So the experience is described as what it is: a one-person practice with written rules and gates. That is a real version of the same problem at a smaller scale, it is stated plainly, and it makes the recommendations credible instead of borrowed.

## Why the brain line is the close

She uses the metaphor herself, so the comment is allowed to turn it: a company's processes are habits, and a habit acquired because somebody else had it is not a habit, it's a costume. That is the question she is already asking herself, said back to her in one sentence.

## What this is not

No link, no offer, no mention of engagements. She is a founder asking peers, and the thread will be full of people selling to her.

Not a claim that Google's process was wrong. Most of it exists because a thousand people needed one answer, which is a reason, just not hers yet.
