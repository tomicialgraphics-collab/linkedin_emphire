## Message, paste as is

<!-- gate:body:start -->Page four, since the levels are their own thing. Your three, used as written, plus one you left out.

The fourth is no record, and in the first year it'll be the answer most people get. It has to look like an ordinary answer rather than a failure, or the first hundred buyers will read it as "this paper is bad" when it means nobody has registered this yet. It's also the moment the product sells itself, because the buyer asks the agent to register and the agent has a reason to open an account.

Who looks, one line each.

A buyer wants to know if this is the file she was given, and who stands behind it.

Ask a seller what he wants and it's proof of what he handed over. If he's honest, it's also proof he didn't swap it later.

An agency wants its name on a record, because its name is the product, and it's the thing that becomes liable if it registers a bad file.

A lawyer wants something to put in a file, and one sentence he can say to a client without being wrong.

For a surveyor it's narrower. Is this plan in circulation the one he drew, and can he say plainly that it isn't.

A registry wants to know whether what's circulating agrees with what it holds. It can also decide the product is a rival register and refuse to recognise it.

A bank wants to know if the paper it's lending against is the same paper, and whether a second version appeared after the search.

Then there's the relative disputing the sale, who wants to know what was registered, on what date, by whom. That's the most common Lagos case and the most dangerous reader, because a record that decides nothing can still win an argument.

Four you missed. Start with the family that owns the land under the plot, who are often the only people who can say a sale was never agreed. There's the tenant already living there, who finds out last. Then the buyer of an off-plan unit, trusting a building that doesn't exist yet. And the next buyer in five years, who doesn't know anyone in the chain and has only the record.

Where I think you're wrong, twice.

The land registry is on your list as a reader. It isn't a neutral one. It can treat a private record as a competing registry, and I'd rather ask it a question than have it read about me in a newspaper.

Level 2 assumes the issuer keeps a copy and will register it. Surveyors usually do, since a plan is a numbered object. Law firms often don't. So level 2 will be common for plans and rare for deeds, and a forged deed sits comfortably in that gap.

Your three questions.

Which issuers. One surveyor first, chosen because he's already lost an argument about which version of a plan was current. He has the copy, the number, and a reason to care. A law firm brings deeds and clients, and I'd go second.

Without the registry, yes, and it has to work that way, but I should say what it costs. Without it the ceiling is level 2, and level 2 never becomes ownership. The product says that rather than hinting the registry is coming.

What a checker sees. The level as the headline and the names underneath. A level with no name is an opinion. A name is something a lawyer can call.

Your postcode links came at a good moment. The record needs a stable way to say where a plot is, and I'd rather use a public one than invent a scheme of my own. I haven't read the docs yet, so that's an intention, not a decision. The candle post I read twice, and it's the right question to be asked. A faster way to hand over the same paper would be a better candle, and this only works if it records something that didn't exist before.
