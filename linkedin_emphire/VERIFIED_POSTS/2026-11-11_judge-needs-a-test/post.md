Your eval has two models in it. You only tested one.

If a model decides whether an output passes, your score is not a measurement of the model you are testing. It is a measure of agreement between two models, one of which you never evaluated and whose version you probably did not write down.

That is not an argument against model judges. Human raters carry the same problem and add their own: they drift, they get tired, and two of them reading the same rubric on a Thursday will not agree with each other on Friday. The judge is not the defect. The defect is scoring without ever testing the judge.

Three things you can run this week, on your own data, no new infrastructure:

→ Run the same judge on the same transcript five times and count how often the verdict moves. If it moves, your pass rate is a sampling error wearing a percentage.

→ Swap the order of the two outputs and score again. A judge with a position preference will pick the other one, and every number you have published just became a coin toss you did not record.

→ Pull fifty items, label them yourself, and report judge-human agreement next to the headline result. Two numbers hold each other up. One number is a claim.

Then put four things in every report: the agreement figure, the number of judge runs per item, the judge's exact model string, and the transcript, so somebody else can re-score it without asking you for anything.

The version string matters more than it looks. A judge upgrade three months from now will quietly rewrite your history, and you will not notice, because the dashboard will still say ninety-two.

A score you cannot re-derive is a claim, not a measurement.
