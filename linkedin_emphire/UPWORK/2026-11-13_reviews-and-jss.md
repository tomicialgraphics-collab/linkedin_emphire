# What a review is worth, and what the price does to it

Short answer: both matter, but they are two different systems and only one of them is about money.

The public review is the thing that sells the next job. Any paid contract can produce one, including a $150 scan.
The Job Success Score is the thing that decides whether a client ever sees the profile. It weighs contract value, and it
does not exist at all until two contracts close with two different clients.

## The two systems, side by side

| | Public review | Job Success Score |
|---|---|---|
| Who sees it | every visitor, in full | every client, as one number under the name |
| What it is | stars plus written feedback, kept forever on the profile | a percentage, recalculated daily, over 6, 12 and 24 month windows |
| What feeds it | the client's rating at close, if they leave one | public rating, private rating the client never shows you, the end reason, contract value, and relationship length |
| Does the price of the job change it | no. a five star review from a $150 job looks exactly like one from a $2,500 job | yes, by a reported factor of 1x, 1.25x or 1.5x depending on the earnings band |
| Minimum before it exists | one | two closed contracts, two different clients, inside 24 months |

Upwork publishes the shape of the second column and not the weights. The banding that keeps appearing in freelancer
write ups, and that matches what people report when a single large contract moves the number more than several small
ones, is this:

| Contract earnings | Reported weight |
|---|---|
| $1 to $250 | 1 job |
| $251 to $1,000 | 1.25 jobs |
| over $1,000 | 1.5 jobs |

Treat the numbers as direction rather than arithmetic. The direction is the part that matters: a $1,500 contract with a
happy client pulls the score half again as hard as a $150 one, and no small contract pulls it as hard as a long one.

## Where the price genuinely does nothing

A review from a $150 job is a review. It carries the same stars, the same written text, the same "verified payment"
marker and the same position on the profile. A client comparing three freelancers does not discount a five star review
because the job was small, and most never open the contract history to check. The price shows in one place, total earned
at the top of the profile, and that number is the weakest thing on the page next to the stars.

So the cheap first job is not a cheap review. It is a real one.

## Where the price matters more than people expect

Two places, and neither is the one people worry about.

1. **The weighting above.** A $600 test instead of a $150 scan puts the same satisfaction into a heavier band. That is the
   argument for the consultation priced at $75 to $95 rather than $30: it lands in the same band, but it does not read
   as a free call with a payment step.
2. **What the buyer believes before they read anything.** At $100 plus earned across two jobs, a stranger assumes a
   person who has been paid twice. At $4,000 they assume a person who is hired. That is a display effect, not a scoring
   effect, and it is why the first two closes matter more than the size of either.

## The trap in small jobs, which is not the price

The real cost of a cheap contract is not the stars, it is the silence. A client who pays $80 for a scan is the client
most likely to take the report and close the tab without leaving anything. A single quiet close does almost nothing. A
run of them dilutes the positive record and, in the reading most freelancers report, starts to look like weak
engagement.

Small is fine. Small and unclosed is not. The close is the work.

## What this means for this account, today

The profile shows $100 plus across two jobs and no score, because nothing has closed. So the target is not a bigger
number, it is two closes with two different clients. The fastest pair available:

1. **The consultation**, booked and delivered, with the client closing it as job completed successfully.
2. **The $150 scan**, with a different client, delivered in three days and closed the same way.

That pair puts a number under the name. Everything after it argues from a score instead of from nothing, and the $600
and $1,500 tiers then pull the number in the heavier band.

## The close-out message, sent on the last day of every contract

Send it in the contract room, after the deliverable is accepted and before the client disappears:

```
The report is delivered and I have nothing outstanding on my side. If it did what you needed, closing the contract as job completed successfully helps me more than anything else you could do here, and a line about what you were trying to find out helps the next person decide whether this is the right test for them.

If something is still open, tell me now rather than closing on it.
```

Three things that message does without asking for a favour: it names the end reason, it asks for feedback in the client's
own interest rather than yours, and it gives the client an exit if something is wrong, which is the only way to hear
about a problem before it becomes a private score.

## Which one, right now

The review, and specifically the close. A score does not exist until two contracts close with two different clients, so
price has nothing to act on yet.

There is one rule worth keeping from this: **inside a price band, a discount buys nothing.** A $150 scan and a $40 scan
both weigh 1x in the score, and both produce the same stars. Discounting from $150 to $40 therefore loses $110 and
changes nothing except how the buyer reads the offer. The bands are $1 to $250, $251 to $1,000, and over $1,000, so the
only price moves that mean anything are the ones that cross a band: $250 up to $300, $600 up to $1,000, $1,000 up to
$1,500.

Sequence on this account: close two contracts at the current prices, $150 and the consultation, with two different
clients. Then price stops being a question of what the market will bear and becomes what the score will carry.

## Three 50 dollar payments, and what they add up to

The first thing to settle is whether those are three milestones on one contract or three contracts. The answer changes
what the client can even leave.

**Feedback is per contract, not per milestone.** A milestone approval releases money. The review appears when the
contract ends, on the end-of-contract form. So three milestones inside one contract produce one review at the close, no
matter how many times the client was pleased along the way.

| Shape | Money net of the 10 percent fee | Public reviews | Weight in the score |
|---|---|---|---|
| one contract, three 50 dollar milestones | 135 | 1, at the close | one outcome, 1x |
| three contracts, one client | 135 | up to 3, one per contract | still one client relationship, so the relationship count does not triple. Roughly one outcome, 1x |
| three contracts, three clients | 135 | 3 | three outcomes at 1x, and this is the shape that makes a score appear at all |

Two things the arithmetic hides.

1. **The score is relationship weighted, not contract weighted.** Ten small contracts with one happy client do not behave
   like ten separate outcomes. Three contracts with one client is one relationship that happened three times.
2. **A run of 50 dollar reviews is visible.** Contract values sit in the work history next to the stars. Three 50 dollar
   reviews read as a person who has been paid three times, which is honest and also small. One 300 dollar review reads as
   a person who has been hired.

The 90 day bonus does not rescue this either: it needs a payment every 90 days on one running contract, not three
payments inside a week.

### What to do instead, when a client wants to pay in instalments

Put the same money on one contract, and put the boundary at a band. A single 250 dollar contract is one clean outcome,
one review, and the top of the 1x band. A single 300 dollar contract is 1.25x. Three 50 dollar contracts are none of
those things, and they cost three times the admin for the same 135 dollars.

If the client genuinely wants to pay per piece, keep it one contract with milestones and close it once, at the end, in
writing.
