# Post 33: the Nvidia resignation, and what actually moved the board

Slot **09:00 WAT, Friday 13 November 2026**. The case study post took the evening of 12 November instead, because
the client cleared it at 20:13 and asked for the location. The news is a day older at this slot and the post still

## Verification: what is established, what is reported, what is not established

- **Established.** Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive, president and board member of Nvidia,
  effective immediately, announced by the board on Thursday 12 November 2026, ending 33 years. Colette
  Kress, the chief financial officer, is interim chief executive. The board's statement thanked him and
  did not mention the reason. Shares fell about 10 percent and the company's market value dropped below
  4 trillion dollars for the first time since 2025.
- **Established.** On Sunday 8 November the board said it had confidence in him and opened an independent
  investigation. It hired a law firm. The board met on Wednesday night to consider removing him. The
  resignation followed on Thursday morning.
- **Reported, and in part acknowledged by him.** The conduct at the centre of this: an admitted
  extramarital affair, stated to staff in an email on Sunday, and further relationships reported by the
  New York Times and Reuters. One of the women is described by United States intelligence officials as an
  intelligence asset. That is an assessment by officials, not a finding by any court.
- **Not established.** Whether any information was compromised, and whether he knowingly assisted any
  foreign intelligence service. Neither has been established, and the post says so rather than implying
  otherwise.
- **Also open.** The FBI and congressional investigations, a shareholder class action, and his expected
  testimony.

Sources read: The New York Times live coverage of the resignation on 12 November 2026, The Guardian live
coverage the same day, Al Jazeera's explainer and news report the same day, and NPR's report. Each supports
the resignation, the interim appointment, the share move and the sequence of Sunday to Thursday.

The post does not name the women, does not repeat the alleged correspondence of any third party, and does
not treat any allegation about a private individual as settled. A governance post that blurs the status of
its own claims is committing the failure it is describing.

<!-- gate:body:start -->
Nvidia's board said on Sunday it had confidence in its chief executive.
By Thursday he was gone. What changed in those four days was who could read the record.

Jensen Huang resigned on 12 November, effective immediately, after 33 years. Colette Kress, the finance chief, is interim chief executive. The board's statement thanked him and gave no reason. Shares fell about 10 percent and the company's value dropped below 4 trillion dollars for the first time since 2025.

Now look at what changed between Sunday and Thursday. The board did not discover a new standard of conduct. It had already opened an investigation and hired a law firm. Then a second report landed, the board met on Wednesday night, and it had his resignation by morning.

So the standard was never the binding constraint. Readership was. An intelligence assessment, a newsroom, a wire service, then a second wire report: each one put material in front of people who did not work for Nvidia and could act on it.

I test systems for a living, and this is the failure I look for. Not whether a standard exists, or whether an investigation was opened, but whether anyone outside the process can obtain the evidence and do something with it. A control only its owner can invoke is a preference with a filing cabinet.

On status, because it matters: the resignation, the appointment and the share move are facts. The conduct behind them is reported, and part of it he acknowledged to staff. Whether any information was compromised has not been established by anyone. A governance post that blurs that line is committing the failure it describes.

A 33-year tenure ended in four days, and what moved it was not a board deciding to look. It was the record becoming readable from outside.
