# Call sheet

You join at 00:39 WAT. He joins at 18:44 his time. Thirty minutes, and it is 18:18 in New York now.

He is 74, he asked for simple English and examples, and he will talk for four minutes at a time if you let him. That is fine for the first eight minutes and expensive after that.

## Leave with three sentences, written down while he talks

1. The one requirement of the 45 that makes the others pointless: ______________________
2. What week four ends in: ______________________
3. The name of the first surveyor: ______________________

If you get those three, the call worked. Everything else can be an email.

## Minute by minute

**0 to 2. Open and set the frame.** Say this close to word for word:

"Marvin, thirty minutes, so I'll be blunt about how I want to use them. Ten on the product, ten on the business, ten on what the practice build actually is. I'm taking notes, and I'll send you the three things I heard within the hour."

Then stop talking. Do not summarise his message back to him.

**2 to 10. His product, not yours.** Two questions, and nothing else from you.

"What's the one requirement of the 45 that, if it fails, makes the other 44 pointless?"

"Of the 45, which eleven are cycle one?"

While he answers, ask one follow-up only: "who checks the verifier?" If he says the verifier is a lawyer or a surveyor, ask how the product shows that person's relationship to the family, because a verifier who is somebody's cousin makes every attestation above him weaker. This is the sharpest thing you know and it is his idea, extended.

**10 to 18. The four things you sent, said in one breath each.** Do not re-read your message.

"Four risks I'd design for. A recording lives on one phone, so if the phone dies the family's only account dies with it. A recording made after a dispute opens is weaker than one made before, and the record can say which. A witness who recants needs a withdrawn state, because a record that hides a withdrawal is worse than none. And a phone walk is five to ten metres out, so it's an event with an author, not a boundary."

Then one sentence, and mean it: "I'd rather find these now than at user fifty."

**18 to 24. Business. Two questions, no opinions on his numbers.**

"Who pays at the moment of the sale?" Expected answer: the agency. Then: "should there be a per-deal price as well as the monthly, because a small agency doing two deals a month won't pay fifteen thousand?" Then the ask: "who's the first surveyor? A name, even if he hasn't agreed yet."

**24 to 29. The week-four sentence.** This is the one you cannot leave without saying. Say it once, plainly:

"One thing I need to say out loud, and then we move on. Four weeks of building for a Claude Pro subscription isn't a fee and I'm not going to pretend it is. I'll do the four weeks because I want to see how you review. What I want is for week four to end in a decision: what the next four cost and who pays, or a clean stop. I'm not asking for a number tonight. I'm asking that the number exists by then."

If he agrees, say "then that's settled" and move on immediately. Do not elaborate, and do not let him negotiate it down in the same breath.

**29 to 30. Close.**

"So: send the documents, send the eleven, and I'll come back with cycle one scoped in a page. Same time next week?"

## What you present

Nothing on screen unless he asks. This is a design conversation with a man who has been building for fifty years, and a demo of someone else's product is the fastest way to look like a contractor.

If he does ask to see something, you have one thing worth ninety seconds: the verifier that compared 16 of 64 hash characters and passed fifty tests, then the same command after the fix. It is the only artefact you own that shows a check which can fail, which is the whole argument of the certificate he wants to build.

## Six questions he may ask, and the honest short answers

"How long would it take you?" Four weeks for eleven requirements is honest, and I'd rather give you eleven that work than forty-five that mostly work.

"What have you built like this?" Nothing with land. I've built checks that make software prove it did what it claimed, and that's the same problem in a different costume.

"Can you do it alone?" Yes for the build. No for the law, the survey or the Yoruba consent wording, and that's three of the people in your plan, not three of mine.

"Will you sign something?" Yes, and I'd rather sign one page that says what happens at the end than five that say what happens if it goes wrong.

"Do you want to be paid?" Yes. That's why the week-four conversation matters, and why I'm not asking for money tonight.

"What do you think of the name?" Covered, and short: the tone marks make it untypeable in an agency office, so keep it working and name it when a user has to say it.

## Do not do these

Do not agree to 45 requirements in four weeks. Do not name a price, a split, an equity number or a fee for the four weeks, on the call or in writing. Do not demo unless asked. Do not say "I'll try"; say what you'll do or say you don't know. Do not let the call run past forty minutes, because he will keep going and you have a busier day than he does.

## Within the hour after

One short message with the three sentences you wrote down, and nothing else. It becomes the record both of you work from, and it is the reason the next call starts where this one finished.
