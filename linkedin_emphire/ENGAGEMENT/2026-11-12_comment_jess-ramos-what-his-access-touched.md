## Comment, paste as is

Whatever the investigation concludes, the part of this that will never be audited is what his access touched.

If a person close to a chief executive is alleged to be a foreign asset, the exposure question isn't what he said to her. It's which documents he could read, which of them left the building, and what a counterparty's staff saw in the ordinary course of a customer relationship. Roadmap, unannounced silicon, export-control strategy, the deal file.

Almost nobody can answer that about their CEO. Engineers sit behind DLP, session logs and egress rules, because that population gets monitored. The one person who can read everything sits behind an assistant and a policy document, on the reasonable grounds that he is the most trusted person in the building.

So the independent investigation can settle conduct, and it cannot settle exposure. Exposure is the part with a security question attached to it.

## Why this angle

Her post is doing two things: an argument about consequences, and a workplace power argument. Both are about conduct, and the thread will fill with conduct. The one thing a security account can add is the distinction between conduct and exposure, because the board has hired a law firm to establish the first and has no instrument for the second.

The specifics matter more than the principle. A chief executive can read the roadmap, the unannounced silicon, the export-control strategy and the deal file, and none of those pass through the logging that covers everybody else in the building. That asymmetry is deliberate and usually correct. It is also the reason a national security question about this cannot be answered by the company's own records, which is a stronger and less comfortable point than the one the thread is making.

The comment says nothing about the women, nothing about the marriage, and nothing about whether he should have resigned. Those are all in her post and none of them is what this account is for.

## Why not skip it

This is a celebrity story on the surface and a governance story underneath, and the account comments on governance. Skipping it would be defensible on taste; the reason to take it is that the useful question is one nobody else in the thread can ask.

What would make me skip instead: if the post had alleged something the reporting does not support, or if the comment could not avoid repeating a claim as if it were established. Neither applies here, and the front matter records what the coverage actually says.
