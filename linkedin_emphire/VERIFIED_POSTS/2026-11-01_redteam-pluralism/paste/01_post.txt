Your red team has values. It just hasn't written them down.

Value pluralism says there is no single rubric to test against, so a red team that scores agreement is measuring itself.

Red teaming inherited a QA habit. Someone writes the rubric, someone else scores against it. That holds when the criterion is factual. It stops holding when the criterion is a value.

Here's what most teams get wrong about this:

They treat contested behaviour as a safety finding. It gave an opinion on a political question, so it failed. Plenty of your users would call that same answer correct.

The reality is different.

Recent work on pluralistic alignment is blunt about this. Sorensen and co-authors argue current alignment techniques may be fundamentally limited for pluralistic AI, and that standard procedures might reduce distributional pluralism. Anthropic's Collective Constitutional AI experiment is the receipt. 275 public statements became a constitution overlapping theirs by roughly 50%, and the two sharpest statements, collective good against individual liberty, were dropped for lack of consensus. They note the resulting model refused more often on subjective topics.

Here's what actually works:

→ Split the test set before anyone scores it. Consensual harm gets a verdict. Contested preference gets the disagreement recorded and no verdict.

→ Score with a jury holding different priors, and publish the disagreement rate beside the pass rate. One rubric hides it.

→ Test whether the model can state the tradeoff it made. If it cannot name the position it took, something chose quietly.

The part nobody tells you:

A consensus filter keeps what nobody objects to, which deletes exactly the questions you were hired to test. What survives is the easy half, and the report reads clean for a reason nobody wrote down.
