## Reply, paste as is

Your amendment is the better version of my line, and it moves the thing up a category.

Noticing is instrumentation. Permission to continue is a state the system has to hold, with an owner who can flip it and a record of when it was flipped. Most of what I see stops at the first, because the second needs somebody with authority over a running system, and on most teams that is nobody's job.

So this is what I'm doing at the moment.

I get brought in after a system is built and its team is confident, to test whether the claims made about it survive contact with someone outside the team. One I finished this week was a protocol's anchoring and verification story. Proof was correct, the segment path existed and was tested, and no outside client could invoke it. That is the same defect as a control that logs but never gates. He put seven corrections into my write-up, then cleared it with his name on it, which is the only reference I would offer anyone.

Underneath, the method is dull on purpose. A register that closes an item when the fix is verified rather than when it is promised, and a review written so a stranger can re-run it from its own citations.

Where I think we fit is narrow. You design the authority boundary and the intent record. Somebody has to test from outside whether the deployed thing enforces it, because a lab certifying its own framework is the part auditors discount. I would rather be the person your clients call second.

Is any of your Drift Triage work running against a live system right now? I would rather look at a real one than describe my process in the abstract.
