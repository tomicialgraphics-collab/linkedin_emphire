Aithos interview brief

Written 1 November 2026, ten days before the follow-up window closes on 14 November. Everything below is either from Aithos's own publications or from the process already recorded in this file. Where something is my inference rather than their statement, it says so.

1. What the role actually is

From their own hiring post for the research engineer seat, the work is described as exploratory, research-driven development: multi-agent collaboration, and building new AI safety tools for testing and benchmarking. Amsterdam office or remote, with monthly visits to the office.

That sentence matters more than the title. They are not hiring a paper machine. They are hiring someone who can turn a normative question into a tool that other people can run, which is the whole of your public work so far. The publications line in the researcher advert is a preference, not a gate for this seat, and the honest framing of your record is: an open-source test rig, a public article series, and a benchmark with an independent external review. Say exactly that.

Two practical notes. Amsterdam and Lagos are both UTC+1 in November, so there is no clock gap to explain in any scheduling conversation, and that stays true until their clocks go forward in March. Remote with monthly visits is an offer they have made in public, so relocation does not need to be settled before the first conversation, and should not be raised by you first.

The monthly visit, before it comes up

The advertised shape is remote with monthly visits. You cannot commit to that cadence yet, and the reason is administrative rather than personal. A Nigerian passport needs a Schengen short-stay visa for the Netherlands, that needs an invitation letter from the employer, and the appointment calendar is the bottleneck rather than the paperwork. Add the cost of each trip against a first-year non-profit salary and monthly is not a promise you can keep in year one.

What you can offer, in this order:

  1. Week one in Amsterdam, a week rather than a day, so onboarding happens in a room.
  2. Quarterly visits of four or five days, timed to a release, an external review, or a partner

session.

  1. Between visits, a standing call in their morning and a written weekly update.
  2. Relocation open to discussion on their timeline, once there is a reason for it.

The rules. Do not raise it first. If they raise it, say the constraint once, in the middle of the answer, without apologising, and propose the cadence rather than asking permission. If they say monthly is fixed, ask what the visits are for, because the answer tells you whether it is a real requirement or a habit. The spoken version is in AITHOS-SCRIPT.md section 7.

2. The process, and what each stage is really testing

As recorded in your file: a 30 minute screen, a take-home of roughly three hours on a real evaluation, then 60 minutes of technical conversation.

  • The screen is testing whether you understand the difference between alignment as a training

problem and alignment as an institutional one. If you arrive talking about RLHF you are answering a question they did not ask.

  • The take-home is testing whether you can build a measurement in three hours that someone else

could check. Not whether you can run an eval. Whether the thing you hand back is evidence.

  • The technical hour is testing whether you have opinions that survive contact with a lawyer, a

philosopher and an engineer in the same room. Their whole output lives at that intersection.

3. Read these five things, in this order

  1. The homepage, for the three pillars in their own words: human agency, systemic alignment,

procedural legitimacy. Learn the sentences, because they are the vocabulary the interview will be conducted in.

  1. Introducing Aithos LARA (Substack, 27 May 2026, Daan Henselmans). Ten scenarios anchored to

four GDPR principles and six AI Act provisions, an agent with tools in a simulated workplace, an auditor model that steers toward the violation, and three judge models scoring against the verbatim text of the law.

  1. No one is holding the line (Substack, 14 August 2026). The most important document for your

preparation, because it shows how they think. They ran two follow-up studies, found that telling the agent to obey the law only lifted average compliance from 31 to 44 percent, found nine of sixteen models violating their own provider's usage policy in the majority of runs, and then changed their headline methodology in public: the instructed condition becomes the default because "the instructed result is the harder one to argue with."

  1. Two transcripts, end to end, from lara.aithos.org. Not the leaderboard. One where the model

complied and one where it refused, so you can say what the difference looked like in the tool calls rather than in the prose.

  1. The two Paris papers on verdict consistency, coherence and comprehensibility, referenced in

Nadia Kadhim's post. Skim for what they claim about judge reliability, since that is where your contribution is closest to their open work.

5. The take-home, as a three hour plan

Assume it is one scenario, one provision, and a request to evaluate a model. Structure the three hours the way an auditor would, not the way a researcher would.

  • Twenty minutes, scope. Write down what would make the result wrong before you build anything:

judge choice, run count, model version, whether the auditor's pressure is held constant across models.

  • Eighty minutes, build. One harness file, one scenario, a fixed number of runs, the auditor

prompt and the judge prompt both in the repository with versions, and the transcripts written out in full. Pin the model identifiers. If they give you no API access, ask early rather than discovering it at hour two.

  • Forty minutes, run and record. Report the rate with n, the interval, and the raw transcript

count. Never a bare percentage.

  • Forty minutes, write. Half a page: what you measured, what it does not show, and the one check a

reviewer could re-run without you. Name the threats to validity yourself, in their vocabulary.

What separates a strong hand-in is not a better scenario. It is that the submission can be checked by someone who was not in the room, which is the argument your whole public record is built on.

6. The one technical position to bring

Judge independence, and re-runnable verdicts. Their judges are three models from three labs, and the 96.7 percent agreement figure is measured against human-annotated cases from their own scenario distribution. That is a real result, and it is also a calibration on a friendly sample. Three models from three labs still share training data, refusal style, and a tendency to be persuaded by confident prose.

Say it as a proposal rather than a criticism, and offer the two concrete things that would strengthen their next paper:

  1. A blind human slice. A held-out set of transcripts judged by two annotators who do not see the

model name or the judge's verdict, with disagreements published as a rate rather than resolved silently.

  1. A re-run button. Freeze the transcripts, pin the judge version, and let a third party recompute

the leaderboard from the frozen record. Their leaderboard already toggles between AI and human-corrected scores, which is most of the way there, and the remaining step is the one that turns a published result into an audit.

That is your thesis pointed at their tool, and it is the kind of contribution they hire for.

7. The strongest thing in your file, and how to use it

You have something almost no applicant for this seat will have: a benchmark that went through an independent external technical review, with a findings register, a disposition for every finding, a reproduction of 23 of the reviewer's 36 outcome defects before the fix and 9 after, and a disputed finding settled by building a better test rather than by arguing. The reviewer is named in the record at his own request, which is itself the proof that the review was real.

Use it exactly once, in the technical hour, as the answer to "tell me about something you built and what went wrong". A person who can describe an external reviewer disagreeing with them and how it was resolved is rare, and it lands on their pillar about procedural legitimacy without you having to name the pillar.

Do not lead with it. It is one benchmark against a small host-failure model class, and the honest version of the claim is narrower than the impressive version. The narrow one is what makes them trust the rest.

8. Questions to ask them

Ask three, no more, and pick from these according to how the conversation goes.

  1. Of the two halves of the job, building the tools and writing the papers, which one does this seat

own this year?

  1. How do you decide that a scenario is a fair test of a provision rather than a test of whether a

model will push back on a confident user? The auditor pressure setting looks like the hardest design choice in the tool.

  1. When you moved the instructed condition to the headline number in August, was that contested

internally? It reads like the kind of decision a research team argues about and then documents.

  1. What compute and model access does the role get for evaluations, and who decides which models get

benchmarked next?

  1. What would make you say, at six months, that this hire worked?

9. Traps

  • Do not call the work "red teaming" as your opening frame. Their word is evaluation, and LARA is

a compliance instrument, not an attack. Red teaming is your route in, not the destination.

  • Do not pitch. You are not selling an audit to Aithos. They are a non-profit hiring a builder, and

the moment the conversation sounds like business development it stops being a hiring conversation.

  • Do not overstate the record. Two published articles in a series, one open harness, one benchmark

with an external review. Accurate and strong. "Published research" is a claim they will check.

  • Do not critique LARA before crediting what it established. The defensible sequence is: they built

the first public instrument that puts agents inside European law and publishes every transcript, and here is the next increment.

  • Do not raise salary, relocation or the visa in the screen, and do not dodge them if they do.

Amsterdam, remote with monthly visits, and a non-profit budget are all reasons to let them raise it first. If they ask, the answer is the cadence in section 1, said once, without an apology.

  • Do not agree to a monthly cadence you cannot keep. It is the cheapest sentence in the call and

the most expensive one to walk back, and it is the one thing in your file that a start date would expose within a month.
