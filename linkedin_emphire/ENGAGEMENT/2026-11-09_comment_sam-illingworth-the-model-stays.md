## The angle

Do not dispute the finding. Add the experiment the finding implies and the firm actually needs. Nobody removes the model; the
operational condition is that it stays and is sometimes wrong, and the capability that matters then is detection.

The metaphor he chose makes the point for him. An exoskeleton's failure is not only that the wearer cannot lift unaided. It is
that the frame can be misaligned while carrying the load, and the wearer has no feeling for it.

## Comment, paste as is

The removal test answers a question most organisations never face. Nobody takes the model away on a Tuesday. It stays, and it is occasionally wrong, and the skill that matters then is noticing.

The exoskeleton metaphor cuts both ways on that. The risk is not only that the wearer cannot lift without it. It is that the assist is misaligned and the wearer cannot feel it, because the frame is taking the load.

So the follow-up I would want is the same population, model still open, with a fraction of the outputs silently corrupted. Who catches it, and does the training move that rate? That is the number a firm can act on, and this design does not produce it.

## What not to say

No summary of the study back to him, no challenge to the effect size, and no correction of the metaphor, which is his and is a
good one. No hashtags, no link, and nothing that reads as a plug for evaluation services. The comment proposes an experiment,
which is the only currency in a thread like this.

## Why the slot is worth it

His audience is learning and development leads and people writing AI literacy policy, which is where evaluation budgets actually
get approved. Proposing the next experiment, rather than doubting this one, is how a practitioner gets read as a peer by
researchers.
