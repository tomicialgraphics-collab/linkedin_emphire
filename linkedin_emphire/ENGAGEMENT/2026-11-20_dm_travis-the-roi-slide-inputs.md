## Message, paste as is

<!-- gate:body:start -->
Keep them coming, and I'd take item one with three changes.

The shape is right, and it's right because a CFO already thinks this way. Exposure equals chance times cost.

But the two inputs are not alike. The 5.56 is sourced and defensible, and rounding it to 5 is fine as long as we write "the IBM average" and not "a breach costs". The 20 per cent is the invented one, and it's the input a CFO attacks first, because loss frequency is where deals actually die.

Worse, IBM can't give us that number. Their sample is organisations that have already been breached, and 95 per cent of those had been breached more than once. That is a fact about recurrence among the breached, not a prediction of any particular firm's chance this year. Put it on a slide as an annual probability and someone who knows the report will take the slide apart.

So I'd turn the probability into a question. What do you think your annual chance is, and who signed off on it. Then we're not defending a number we invented, we're testing theirs, and the testing is the product.

There is a second route if they won't answer. 160 dollars a record against the number of records they actually hold lands on their size instead of somebody's average, with the caveat IBM prints itself about very large breaches.

And keep the exchange rate visible on the same slide. If exposure is a million a year and the test is eighteen hundred, we are not selling a million of avoided loss. We are selling the cheapest way to find out whether 20 per cent is 20 per cent and whether 5 million is their 5 million.
