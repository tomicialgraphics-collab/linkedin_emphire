## Why this angle

The post asks whether a founder's track record should buy room or raise the bar. In governance language that is
not a moral question, it is a control question: which control was supposed to act, when, and did it. His readers
are GRC practitioners, so detective versus preventive control is the argument they can use on Monday.

The verified event makes the point better than any framework. Earlier in the week the company said Huang was
committed to leading without interruption. On Thursday he had resigned as chief executive, president and board
member, Colette Kress was interim, and 888 billion in market value had gone. The detective control existed and
worked late. Nothing preventive was in place, so the market moved first.

The last paragraph is the honest one. NVIDIA's operations ran without him, so the machine was mostly real. Saying
so costs nothing and makes the criticism of the missing check credible rather than gleeful.

## Comment, paste this

For anyone in GRC reading this, the case is a control design failure before it is a founder story.

NVIDIA had a detective control: a board that could remove a chief executive once the conduct came out. Earlier in
the week the company was still saying he was committed to leading without interruption. By Thursday he had
resigned and 888 billion of value had gone. The detective control worked. The preventive one did not exist, so the
market acted first and the board followed.

The test I use on any system, and it works on companies: remove the person on Monday morning and see what
actually stops. If the answer is nothing, the person was the control, and there was no compensating control
behind them.

The good news buried in the story is that the operations ran without him, so most of that machine was real. What
was never built is the check that would have acted before the conduct became a share price.
