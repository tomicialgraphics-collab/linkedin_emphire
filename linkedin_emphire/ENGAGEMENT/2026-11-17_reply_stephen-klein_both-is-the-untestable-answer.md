## Comment, paste as is

You may well be right, and it's the answer I'd bet on too.

The awkward part about both is that it looks identical to one or the other from outside. An attack that works is designed to resemble decay, and decay throws off the same timestamps and the same plausible explanations. So both isn't a compromise between two theories. It's the case where the only thing that separates them is a record nobody outside the operator holds.

Which is why I'd put the same question to all six. If the honest answer is thirty days of an operator's own logs and nothing independent, then both is a hypothesis nobody can test, and it gets reported afterwards as a coincidence.

## Why the agreement is the argument

He offered the most defensible position in the thread and it takes one sentence to agree with, so the reply agrees in the first four words and spends the rest on the part that makes it hard rather than the part that makes it comfortable.

Both being true is worse than either for the same reason the original comment gave: it removes the last simple explanation. A coincidence requires one cause. Deliberate and ordinary at once means the only distinguishing evidence is in the logs, and the logs are held by the organisation being asked whether it was attacked.

## Why it stays short

He asked a two word question. A reply longer than the question at this point reads as wanting the last word, and the thread is better served by the argument being complete in three paragraphs than by it being exhaustive.

## If he replies

The question worth having is whether any of the six is regulated by a body that publishes its own finding, because that is the only route to a negative claim that is not authored by the party under examination. The FAA is the candidate.
