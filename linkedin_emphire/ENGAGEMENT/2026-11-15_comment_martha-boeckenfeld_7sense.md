# Comment on Dr Martha Boeckenfeld's 7Sense post, 15 November 2026

What this is: one comment for her post, aimed at the board-level readers she actually has rather than at the
admiration thread. What to do next: paste it once. No link, no pitch.

## Why this angle

Her last line is a request for verification and the comment answers it, which is the only way to add something
under a post like this without sounding like a man at a wedding with a correction. The comments beneath are
almost all delight, plus one person asking whether it is braille for the forehead.

The contribution is the evaluation criterion that matters for a device standing in for a sense: the miss, not
the false alarm. A false alarm is self-correcting in a week. A miss is silent, happens once, and costs a fall.
That asymmetry is the same one a red team tests for, so it is the owner's own discipline applied to somebody
else's hardware, and it does not require any knowledge of assistive technology to state.

Her 2:1 principle is used once, at the end, because it turns the comment into a sentence she can quote. The
capability is already funded. The trial is the governance spend, and it is the cheapest part of the purchase.

The tone is deliberate: the capability is called impressive and funded before the gap is named, and nothing is
said about what blind users should want. The test proposed is for the buyer, not for the wearer.

## Comment, paste this

Verifiable is the right word, and it's the one the price has to answer.

A device that stands in for a sense is judged on what it misses, not on what it announces. A false alarm is
self-correcting: the wearer learns to discount it within a week. A miss is silent and happens once, and the cost
is a fall or a vehicle. So the number that would justify 9,000 euros to me isn't the 15 millisecond latency or
the 185 degree field of view. It's how many obstacles went unsignalled in a week of ordinary walking, on a route
the company didn't choose.

That asymmetry is the one we test for in software, and it's why a demo proves so little. The happy path shows the
thing works when nothing goes wrong. What you want is the record of everything it failed to catch, kept by
someone who isn't selling it.

Your 2:1 principle fits this better than most AI. The capability side is already impressive and already funded.
The human judgment side is the trial that says what it misses, and a buyer spending four figures on a sense
should ask for that before the rest of the money moves.
