# Sell Like Crazy, end to end, and the six things this account still is not doing

Read this pass: the whole file at `books/sell-like-crazy.md`, all eight phases, plus the two earlier applications already in
the repo (`STRATEGY/2026-10-11_book-frameworks_upwork-listings-linkedin-strategy.md` and
`LINKEDIN/sell_like_crazy_5k_playbook_2026-10-25.md`). This note records only what those two did not already take, so
nothing here repeats the Godfather Offer skeleton, the HVCO reasoning or the traffic arithmetic that are already applied.

## 1. The one sentence that names the gap

The book's central claim is not that you need better copy. It is that you need an **offer**, and an offer is two things:
what the buyer wants when they respond, and what they have to do to get it.

What this account has is a **price list**: three tiers, three delivery times, three descriptions. A price list answers
"how much". An offer answers "why is refusing this stupid". Those are different objects, and the difference is the whole
book.

Suby's line for it: if the offer and the guarantee would not keep the founder up at night, they are not strong enough.
Nothing on this account currently keeps anybody up at night. That is the finding.

## 3. Lever one: the Power Guarantee, which the account does not have

The book spends more pages on this than anything else after the offer itself, and the argument is simple: no guarantee
means the buyer carries all the risk, and a stranger will not carry it for someone they met four minutes ago. Upwork's
escrow covers non-payment. It does not cover **disappointment**, and disappointment is what a buyer of a security test is
actually afraid of.

The fear, in the buyer's words, is one of two things:

1. "I pay $600 and get a PDF that lists forty vague issues, none of which I can reproduce, so I cannot fix anything or show
   anyone."
2. "I pay $600 and the report tells me what I already knew, so I have bought nothing."

A guarantee has to hit one of those and be genuinely survivable for you. Both of these are, because both are already your
doctrine.

**For the $600 and $1,500 tiers:**

```
Every finding in the report comes with the prompt, the model revision and the date, so you can run it yourself and get the same result.

If any finding in the report cannot be reproduced from what I give you, I strike it and replace it with a new test at no charge, and if there is nothing left to strike it out with, you get the money back.

One guarantee I will not make: that a system is safe. Nobody can make that one, and anyone who does is selling you a certificate, not a test.
```

That last paragraph is the strongest part. It refuses the guarantee every competitor offers and turns the refusal into
proof of competence. It is also true.

**For the consultation:**

```
If the call does not leave you with a written list you can act on this week, tell me inside 24 hours and it is refunded.
```

**Where it goes:** the last third of each catalog description, and the first thing after the price in the DM handoff.
Upwork's own refund mechanics make it enforceable, which is what makes it credible rather than decorative.

## 4. Lever two: genuine scarcity, which costs you nothing because it is real

The book insists scarcity must be genuine, and warns that fake countdowns get punished. You have real scarcity and you
have never said it out loud: **one person, two four-layer runs a week, three days each**. The calendar is the constraint,
not demand.

```
Two four-layer tests a week, because each one takes three days of my own time and I do the work myself. If the calendar is full when you book, you go on the list for the following week and I will tell you the date before you pay.
```

That sentence does four things: it caps supply honestly, it explains the cap in a way that implies quality, it removes
the fear of being queued silently, and it gives the buyer a reason to decide this week without a fake deadline.

If you want a second scarcity element, use a dated price change rather than a countdown: **prices for new clients go up on
1 January 2027**, and say why (the number of tests per week does not change, so the price has to). That is checkable and
it is true for as long as you decide it is.

## 5. Lever three: the premium stack, which is currently sold as a $1 line item

The book's rule on premiums: they raise perceived value without discounting the price, and the buyer must be able to see
the stack. Right now your best premium is priced at **$1** as an add-on ("14-day re-test"). A $1 premium signals that it
is worth a dollar.

What is actually free for you to give and expensive for them to buy elsewhere:

| Premium | What it costs you | What it is worth to them |
|---|---|---|
| 20 minute findings walkthrough, recorded | 20 minutes | being able to explain the finding to a colleague who was not there |
| One page executive summary written for a board or a client | 30 minutes | the thing they forward, which is how the report gets read |
| 14 day verification re-test of the fixes | an hour of re-running | proof that the fix held, which is what procurement asks for |
| The 168-class coverage checklist filled in for their system | already public, ten minutes to fill | the "what was not tested" page, which nobody else sends |

Present them as included, with a stated total, and never as a discount:

```
Included at this price: the findings walkthrough call, a one page summary written for whoever signs off, the filled coverage page showing what was not tested, and a verification re-test 14 days after you ship the fixes.
```

Then the $1 add-on goes away, because selling your most valuable premium for a dollar is the clearest sign in the whole
profile that the seller has not priced the offer.

## 6. Lever four: the back end, which is where the book says the money is

Phase 8 is blunt: the first sale is the cheapest sale you will ever make, and the profit is in what happens after it. This
account has no second sale. Every catalog item is a one-off, which means every client is worth one invoice and then leaves.

The second sale for a red team test is not another test. It is **the same test, run again, on a schedule**, because the
thing that broke last month breaks again when someone edits a prompt. That is a subscription-shaped product and it can sit
inside a fixed-price catalog as a three month block.

```
Monthly regression re-test, three months: $600

The same four-layer run, once a month for three months, on the current revision. Each month you get the findings delta: what is new, what came back, what is closed. Priced at $200 a month because the scope does not change between runs.
```

Why this matters more than any copy change: one client who buys the $600 test and the $600 block is a **$1,200** client, and
the second half needs no new customer, no connect and no proposal. Two of those a quarter is the entire gap to the target,
and it is the only lever in this document that compounds.

## 7. Lever five: the LinkedIn opt-in, which the book says is the missing half of the giveaway

Phase 2 and Phase 3 together: give something valuable, and **capture contact details in exchange**. You do the first half.
The playbook is public, it is good, and nobody has to give you anything to read it. That is generosity with no mechanism.

LinkedIn's only capture mechanism is the DM. So the asset gets a second life as a **request**, which is the honest
equivalent of an opt-in page:

```
I have the 168-class checklist as a one page PDF, filled in for your system if you tell me what it does. Comment "checklist" or message me and I will send it.
```

Two rules for it: it goes in the **first comment**, never the post body, and it is sent by hand, one at a time, because a
bulk send is the thing that gets accounts restricted.

What the DM then does is the Magic Lantern in miniature, four touches over twelve days, and it is the only nurture system
this account has:

| Day | Message | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | the checklist, plus one line about what usually comes back unfilled | deliver, and earn the next message |
| 3 | one finding from a real test, no ask | prove the work is real |
| 7 | "the page for your system is missing [class]. that one is worth a look before you ship." | specific, useful, no pitch |
| 12 | one sentence, one link: the $150 scan if they want it done properly | the only ask, once |

## 8. Lever six: your connects are an ad budget, and should be judged like one

Phase 5's contribution is not "buy ads". It is the demand that you know your cost per customer before you spend, and that
you kill a channel that cannot pay for itself.

You are already spending. 84 connects on hand, about 12 per proposal, so seven proposals. The book's logic applied:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| connects per proposal | about 12 |
| connects on hand | 84 |
| proposals fundable | 7 |
| proposal to interview, reported industry range | 1 in 5 to 1 in 10 |
| interview to hire at this account's stage | unknown, treat as 1 in 3 |
| cost per client, in connects | roughly 150 to 360, which is more than you have |

That last line is the honest finding: **at 84 connects you cannot afford a client through proposals alone.** Which is why
the catalog and the Featured section matter more than the proposal engine, and why the first thing to fix is not a
proposal. This is arithmetic on assumptions, not a measurement, and it should be replaced with your own numbers after the
first ten sends.

Kill rule, so the channel is judged rather than believed: **if 20 proposals produce no interview, the proposal copy is the
problem, not the connects.** If 20 produce interviews and no hires, the offer is the problem. Those are different fixes and
the book's discipline is to know which one you are in.

## 9. The rebuilt $600 offer, all seven parts, paste-ready

This is the same product as the current listing. Only the offer around it changes.

**1. Rationale.** "I run two of these a week because I do the work myself. The price is what it is because a test that
finds nothing still takes three days."

**2. Build value.** "A penetration test on a web app runs $3,000 to $10,000 and does not cover the model. The four-layer
test covers prompt injection, tool abuse, retrieval poisoning and permission drift, and it costs $600 because it is scoped
to one feature rather than one company."

**3. Price.** $600, one feature, seven days. The $150 scan exists for the buyer who is not sure yet, and the $1,500 tier
exists for the buyer who wants the fixes done rather than described.

**4. Payment.** For the $1,500, three milestones of $500: scope, test, fixes. Nobody pays $1,500 before the first finding
lands.

**5. Premiums.** The four in section 5, stated as included.

**6. Guarantee.** The one in section 3, including the refusal.

**7. Scarcity.** Two slots a week, and the queue date is given before payment.

**The first line of the description, which is the only line most people read:**

```
Find out what your AI feature does when someone who is not your user talks to it, in seven days, with every finding reproducible.
```

## 10. What I am not taking from the book

- **Paid traffic.** Phase 5 assumes a funnel with a measured cost per lead. There is no budget and no measurement yet, and
  the earlier note already gate-kept this.
- **A countdown timer.** Fake urgency is the one thing in the book that would cost more than it earns here, because the
  audience is technical and checks.
- **Discounting to fill the calendar.** The band arithmetic in `UPWORK/2026-11-13_reviews-and-jss.md` shows a discount
  inside a band buys nothing.
- **The full email funnel.** The list does not exist. LinkedIn DMs are the substitute and they have to stay manual.
