## Comment, paste as is

The hardest thing to hand over isn't the knowledge, and the questions in your post point at it without landing on it. Head knowledge goes quiet inside a week and someone rebuilds it. Standing doesn't move at all. What a 33-year chief executive actually held was the authority to say no: kill a programme, absorb a customer exception, overrule the roadmap in a room where nobody else could.

An interim appointment transfers the title in an afternoon. None of that comes with it, so the person now holding the title spends the first year borrowing credibility instead of spending it.

The succession test I'd add to yours is who is allowed to refuse, and whether anyone else has ever done it out loud.

## Why this rather than agreement

His post is good and it makes the move most people miss, which is to turn a story about a four trillion dollar company into a question about a team of six. Agreeing with that adds nothing to him or to anyone reading.

What the comment does is separate the two things his questions run together. He asks who knows, who decides and who would make the call. The first is knowledge and it is recoverable. The second is authority and it is not, because authority in a company is not a document, it is a history of decisions other people watched you make and accepted.

That distinction is also the one that makes his own example sharper. The board named an interim in a day. What nobody can name is the moment the interim has the standing to overrule the roadmap, and that is a year, not a signature.

## If he replies

The question worth having is whether a small team can create the second thing on purpose, because the honest answer is that standing is granted by a group over time and can be given deliberately by a founder who says out loud, in front of everyone, that a named person can refuse.
