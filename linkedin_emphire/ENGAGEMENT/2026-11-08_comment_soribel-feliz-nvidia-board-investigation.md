# Comment on Soribel Feliz's Nvidia post, 8 November 2026

## Why this one

She is the closest thing to a direct peer in the feed this week: someone who is paid to make governance survive board
examination and legal review. Her post is a link and two hashtags, so there is no argument to answer, and the comment
section will fill with jokes about a divorce and moralising about the affair. Both are easy and neither is her subject.

The comment treats the board statement as a governance artefact and reads it the way she would read a client's policy:
what does it commit to, what can the process conclude, and what will be quotable in six months.

## Comment

What's worth reading closely is the statement's own structure. It opens an investigation and, in the same breath, says the
board has no reason to believe secrets were compromised and has confidence in the leadership. That may be true, but it's a
conclusion published before the process meant to reach it. The committee will now be read as either confirming the board
or contradicting it.

The wording that matters in six months is the scope. "All available information" isn't a scope. It doesn't say what the
committee can compel, what it must publish, or whether "we could not determine" is an available finding. Those three are
what separate an investigation from a press release.

## What not to say

No joke about the affair or the divorce framing. No speculation about the woman named in the reporting. No prediction
about his job, the stock or the China export policy. Each of those is the comment everyone else is writing, and the
people who hire her read pile-ups.

## Facts held back on purpose

The House Foreign Affairs closed-door briefing reported for 18 November appears in secondary coverage only, so it is not
used. The earlier Nvidia line describing the contacts as routine meetings with a regional cloud customer is accurate and
quotable, but it belongs to a different argument about corporate statements, and adding it would turn one observation
into an accusation.
